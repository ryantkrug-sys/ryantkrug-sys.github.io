1. The Digital SAT at a Glance

The SAT is now fully digital. Your child takes it on a laptop or tablet (their own or a school-issued one) using the College Board's Bluebook app, usually at a school or test center on a Saturday morning. Many students also take it at school on a weekday (SAT School Day), and Sunday dates exist for students who can't test on Saturday.

  • Scores run from 400 to 1600 — 200 to 800 for each section, added together.
  • College Board benchmarks: the College Board sets a college-readiness benchmark for each section — 480 for Reading & Writing and 530 for Math. A benchmark score signals that a student is on track for college and career readiness: students who reach it have about a 75% chance of earning at least a C in related first-year college courses. Scoring below the benchmark doesn't mean a student can't succeed in college — it means those skills need more work before they get there.
  • A graphing calculator is built into the test. The Bluebook app includes the Desmos calculator for the entire Math section, so there's no need to buy an expensive calculator.
  • There is no penalty for guessing. A wrong answer costs exactly the same as a blank one, so your child should answer every single question, even if it's a pure guess in the last ten seconds.
  • Reading & Writing looks different from what you remember. Instead of a few long passages, most questions come with their own short passage — often just a paragraph — and a few questions work with a pair of short passages.

2. What "Adaptive" Actually Means

This is the single biggest change from the paper SAT, and the part most parents don't hear about until after the first score comes back.

Each section is split into two modules. Module 1 contains a mix of easy, medium, and hard questions. How your child does on Module 1 decides what Module 2 looks like:

  • Do well on Module 1, and Module 2 is harder — and the highest scores become reachable.
  • Struggle on Module 1, and Module 2 is easier — but the highest score your child can earn is capped at a lower level, no matter how many of those easier questions they get right.

Two practical consequences. First, a score is not just a percentage. Two students can answer the same number of questions correctly and receive noticeably different scores, because the difficulty of the questions matters too. Second, the first module of each section deserves your child's sharpest focus — careless mistakes in Module 1 can cost more than the same mistakes later, because they can decide which version of Module 2 your child gets. The good news: students who know this going in handle the test very differently from students who don't.

3. A Realistic Timeline

Here's the schedule that works for most families. The goal of the whole timeline is simple: have a solid SAT score in hand before senior year starts, so the fall of senior year is free for applications and essays — not test prep.

  • Summer after sophomore year — the best time to start reviewing. A few weeks of light, steady work the summer before junior year — before school, sports, and application pressure pile up — pays off all year. Use it to shore up the basics and take a first diagnostic.
  • October of junior year — the PSAT/NMSQT. Most high schools offer it to juniors in October. Many schools cover the cost, though some charge a fee, so check with your school. Treat it as a low-stakes preview of the real thing — it's the best diagnostic you'll get. High scorers in their junior year can qualify for National Merit recognition, with cutoffs varying by state.
  • Spring of junior year — the first real SAT. March, May, and June are the popular dates. Spring of junior year is ideal: the material is fresh, and there's still time to retake.
  • Summer after junior year — finish the job. If the spring score isn't where it needs to be, the June and August dates are where the score gets finished. The August test is a favorite — no schoolwork competing for attention — and a solid score there means testing is done before senior year even begins.
  • Fall of senior year — a backup, not the plan. If a fall retake is truly needed, September and October are the remaining options, and the October date is the last comfortable one for early application deadlines. But treat fall testing as a last resort: senior fall is crowded enough with applications and essays, and the point of this schedule is to have the score settled before it starts. Students applying early should check each college's testing deadline.
  • Many students take the SAT two or three times. That's normal and expected — colleges know it, and scores often improve with a second sitting plus focused review in between — though improvement is never guaranteed.
  • Many colleges "superscore" — they take your child's best Reading & Writing score and best Math score from different test dates and combine them. Policies vary, so check each college on your child's list. Superscoring is one more reason a retake is usually worth it.

4. What Actually Moves a Score

After about 15 years of doing this, I can tell you the unglamorous truth: scores don't rise from taking practice test after practice test. They rise from what happens after the practice test.

Reviewing missed questions matters more than raw volume

A practice test taken and never reviewed is just a measurement — it tells you where you stand but does almost nothing to change it. The improvement lives in the questions your child got wrong.

The missed-question method

For every missed question, your child should be able to answer one question: why did I miss it? The honest answer is almost always one of four things — I didn't know the concept, I fell for a trap answer, I misread the question, or I ran out of time. Each cause has a different fix. Then, a few days later, they should redo the question cold, without looking at the solution. Turning missed questions into flashcards (the free SAT Question Deck in the resources below collects them into a flashcard deck automatically) makes this easy to keep up with.

Desmos fluency for Math

The built-in Desmos calculator can graph equations, solve systems, and check answers visually. A student who knows how to use it can solve many algebra problems in a fraction of the time the pencil-and-paper method takes — and catch mistakes before moving on. It's the closest thing to a legal shortcut the test offers.

Reading stamina and the basics

Reading & Writing rewards students who read regularly — the passages are short, but dense. And don't overlook the boring stuff: a full night's sleep — ideally two good nights in a row before the test — breakfast, arriving early, and a charged device with Bluebook already installed and tested.

Simulate the real thing

At least twice before test day, your child should take a full practice test in the Bluebook app, timed, in one sitting, with the break — the same way the real test runs. This does three things: it builds the stamina for a test that runs well over two hours, it makes the software itself boring and familiar (no surprises on test day), and it produces the missed questions that drive the review work described above. One full simulation every couple of weeks during the prep window is plenty; more than that usually means less time for the review that actually raises the score.

The test-day checklist

None of this is complicated, but all of it is easier handled the night before than in a parking lot on Saturday morning:

  • Device ready: the testing device is fully charged, Bluebook is installed, and your child has completed exam setup in the app 1–5 days before test day — completing exam setup is what generates the admission ticket in the app. Most test-day stress comes from software problems that could have been caught and fixed at home. Students who need a device can request a loaner from College Board at least 30 days in advance.
  • Admission ticket and photo ID: once exam setup is complete (days before the test, not at check-in), the admission ticket is available in the app — your child can print it or show it on their device. Make sure they also have an acceptable physical photo ID.
  • Know the logistics: where the test center is, where to park or drop off, and what time doors open. Plan to arrive early; a rushed arrival follows a student into Module 1. Doors close at 8:00 a.m., and late arrivals aren't admitted.
  • Scratch paper: the test center provides it, but your child should bring a pen or pencil.
  • Break supplies: a snack and water for the 10-minute break. No food or drink is allowed during testing itself, so breakfast matters more than usual.
  • Calculator: optional. The built-in Desmos calculator covers the entire Math section, but students who prefer a handheld calculator may bring an approved one.
  • The night before: normal bedtime, everything laid out, alarm set. The night before the test is for sleeping, not for one last practice section.

5. Five Mistakes Parents Make

  1. Taking practice tests without ever reviewing them. Volume feels productive, but unreviewed mistakes just get practiced again. One test reviewed thoroughly beats four tests taken and shelved.
  2. Starting two weeks before the test. Real score gains come from steady work over time, not a cram session. Starting the summer after sophomore year — even at just one session a week — builds skills gradually, long before the pressure is on, and makes the last-minute cram unnecessary.
  3. Ignoring the questions their child got wrong. The missed questions are the curriculum. They show exactly which concepts and traps to work on — no guessing required.
  4. Choosing prep by brand name instead of fit. The best prep is the one your child will actually do, at their current score level, with review built in. A famous logo doesn't guarantee any of that.
  5. Panicking over one score. One test is one morning. Many colleges are test-optional, which means your child chooses whether to send a score at all — but a strong score still genuinely helps with admissions and merit scholarships at many schools, depending on the school. And a growing number of selective colleges require scores again. Either way, it's worth pursuing a strong score without treating any single result as a verdict.

7. When Tutoring Makes Sense

Honestly? Not every student needs a tutor. A motivated student with good free materials and a real review habit can make solid gains on their own — the resources above are a legitimate starting point.

Tutoring tends to be worth it in three situations: your child's score has plateaued despite genuine practice; there's a specific score goal tied to a scholarship or a particular college list; or your child simply won't self-study consistently and needs structure, a plan, and someone to answer to each week.

8. Quick FAQ

How many times should my child take the SAT?

Two or three times is typical. A first sitting in spring of junior year, a retake after targeted review, and a third only if there's a clear reason — a specific score within reach, for example.

If colleges are test-optional, is the SAT still worth it?

Usually, yes. Test-optional means your child can choose whether to submit a score — it doesn't mean scores stopped mattering. A strong score still helps with admissions and merit money at many schools, and having one keeps every option open.

What score is "good"?

It depends entirely on the college list. The national average is around 1030–1040, depending on the year, but the only benchmark that matters is the middle range of scores at the colleges your child is actually applying to — which most colleges publish on their admissions site or in their Common Data Set.

When should prep start?

Aim to start during the summer after sophomore year. At that stage, one session per week is plenty — it builds skills steadily, long before the pressure of junior year, without crowding out school, sports, and everything else. As a target test date gets closer, the pace can pick up if needed — as long as missed questions get reviewed along the way.

My child's score went down on a retake. What now?

It happens, and it's not the disaster it feels like. Many colleges let students choose which test dates to send, and colleges that superscore only ever use the best section scores — a lower sitting simply doesn't count. A few colleges require all scores to be sent, so check each school's policy. Look at the section scores separately: it's common for one section to rise while the other dips, and under superscoring, that's still progress.

A Final Word

The families who get the most out of the SAT treat it as a project with a plan: a target date, steady weekly work starting the summer after sophomore year, honest review of every missed question, and a retake built into the schedule from the start. It doesn't have to take over your household, and it doesn't require the most expensive program on the market.
